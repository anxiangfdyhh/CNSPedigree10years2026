ACADEMIC LINEAGE × FLAGSHIP JOURNALS · DATA ANALYSIS

Who Publishes in Flagship Journals? The Nobel Lineage of CNS Neuroscience Papers (2016–2025)

Over the decade 2016–2025, Nature, Science and Cell together published roughly 3,500 neuroscience research papers. We placed the last (corresponding) author of each paper into the Academic Family Tree and traced the mentor lineage upward: in about 4 of every 5 papers, five generations of mentors lead back to a Nobel laureate — and this share has barely moved in ten years. Among neuroscience researchers within the tree itself (the Neurotree subset of the Academic Family Tree), only 42% can trace such a lineage. Pedigree does not prove paper quality — but it does predict who publishes in flagship journals.
How to read these numbers: ① Papers whose authorship cannot be resolved — ambiguous names, or authors absent from the tree — are excluded rather than guessed (hence "papers with resolvable lineage"); ② The last (corresponding) author is identified as the final author of the byline; ③ The 42% baseline is not a field-wide survey — it is the share among neuroscience researchers within the tree itself (its Neurotree subset); the tree skews toward elite lineages, and the two biases point in opposite directions — both are documented in the Methods at the bottom of the page.

Is it just that descendants are numerous? a per-capita accounting (2016–2025)

Seeing "81%", a natural question follows: is it simply that Nobel lineages contain more people? So here is a different accounting — count people, not papers. Among every 100 neuroscientists in the tree, how many served as last (corresponding) author of a CNS flagship paper in 2016–2025? The answer: in Nobel lineages, 1 in 75 (1.33%); among other scholars, only 1 in 600 (0.17%) — roughly an 8-fold difference. The lineages are indeed large, but the per-capita opportunity is also far greater.

By generation it is even clearer: a laureate's direct students (Gen 1) produce one CNS last author in roughly every 14 people (7.37%), declining with each generation — yet by Gen 5, the rate is still 0.31%, nearly twice the control. The lineage "halo" fades generation by generation, but within five generations it never disappears.

How this accounting works: only scholars tagged as neuroscience in the tree are counted, so numerator and denominator match (869 lineage members served as last authors / 65,429 lineage members in total; others 151 / 90,121). About three in ten CNS last authors come from chemistry, physics and similar backgrounds (untagged as neuroscience in the tree); they fall outside this accounting — they belong to other disciplinary populations, whose rates should be judged against their own population bases. Including them lowers the multiplier from 7.9 to 4.6 — a more conservative bound that leaves the conclusion unchanged. Two further numbers of interest: among neuroscience-background CNS last authors, 85% come from Nobel lineages; among cross-disciplinary ones, 60% do.

Tree snapshot 2025-11-10: lineage members by generation 1,817 / 9,415 / 20,005 / 20,798 / 13,394; corresponding CNS last (corresponding) authors 134 / 299 / 268 / 127 / 41; laureates themselves counted separately. The tree is crowdsourced and coverage is biased; collection intensity may also differ between the two groups, so the multipliers should be read as approximate.

Per-capita flagship-journal rate by generation Nobel descendant generations 1–5 vs non-descendants (2016–2025)

The closer an author sits to a Nobel laureate in the mentoring chain, the higher their per-capita rate of publishing in a flagship journal — declining from 7.37% (Gen 1, a laureate's direct students) to 3.18% (Gen 2) to 1.34% (Gen 3) to 0.61% (Gen 4) to 0.31% (Gen 5), compared with 0.17% among non-descendants. Even at the fifth generation, the rate is still nearly twice the control.

Only neuro-tagged scholars are counted (numerator and denominator matched). Generation = minimum mentor-student distance to the nearest Nobel laureate (BFS upward). Non-descendant control = scholars with no Nobel ancestor within five generations. Population by generation: 1,817 / 9,415 / 20,005 / 20,798 / 13,394; non-descendant 90,121. CNS last authors: 134 / 299 / 268 / 127 / 41 / non-descendant 151.

How many Nobel lineages does a single author belong to? multi-branch analysis (2016–2025)

Most Nobel-descendant CNS last authors trace back to a single Nobel laureate ancestor within five generations. But some belong to two or more independent Nobel lineages simultaneously — their mentors (or mentors' mentors) descend from different laureates. The more branches an author belongs to, the more deeply embedded they are in the Nobel academic network.

Branch count = number of independent mentor spines leading to distinct Nobel laureates within five generations. For each of the author's direct mentors, we trace upward; if that mentor line reaches a Nobel laureate, it counts as one branch. Only the nearest laureate per mentor spine is counted (avoiding network-explosion inflation). Only authors classified as Nobel descendants (Gen 1–5) are counted; laureates themselves are excluded.

Methods and definitions

Corpus: PubMed search for neuroscience papers published in Nature / Science / Cell between 2016-01-01 and 2025-12-31 (MeSH: Nervous System / Brain / Neurons / Neurosciences, or neuro* title/abstract prefix), restricted to research articles (reviews, news and comments excluded), 3,534 papers in total. Of these, 22 (classifiable ones) were published online in late 2025 and assigned 2026 issue dates by PubMed; they are excluded from the statistics by default; including them barely changes the results).

Lineage determination: Anchored on the 744 science Nobel laureates marked award = "Nobel" in the Academic Family Tree (literature, peace, economics and theology prizes excluded), a breadth-first search follows mentor → trainee directed edges; anyone reachable within five generations is classified as a "Nobel descendant", recording the minimum generation; those reachable only at generations six to ten are listed separately as "descendant · other" (68 papers under the strict window), kept out of the five-generation primary share — the 42.1% baseline is likewise a five-generation figure, so mixing them in would be incomparable. A note on data sources: the Academic Family Tree is the umbrella database of academic genealogies across disciplines, and Neurotree is its neuroscience sub-tree. Lineage tracing and author matching in this analysis are performed on the full database (887,736 people) — otherwise Nobel laureates from physics, chemistry and physiology (and their cross-disciplinary mentoring chains) would be missed. The baseline population (42.1%) is restricted to the 155,684 scholars in the full database whose majorarea includes neuro.

Disambiguation: Last (corresponding) authors are matched to tree entries by "name + institution"; difficult cases are re-checked against ORCID employment records. Authors who cannot be uniquely resolved (true name collisions) or are absent from the tree are excluded and enter no denominator (classify-then-analyse). The percentages on this page therefore match the article: 81% refers to the share among classifiable papers.

Baseline: Among the 155,684 tree members whose majorarea includes neuro, five-generation Nobel descendants account for 42.1% — the 81% among flagship-journal last authors is roughly twice this baseline.

Subfield classification: A paper's "subfield" is mapped from its PubMed MeSH major topics by hand-curated keyword rules, taking a single primary label under the priority order "Disease & Clinical > Cognition & Behavior > Development & Aging > Systems & Circuits > Cellular & Molecular > Methods & Computation" (papers often span several categories; only the most informative one is kept). Papers carrying only generic terms such as Brain or Neurons are labelled "Unclassified (generic terms)"; papers without MeSH annotation are labelled "No MeSH tags". Model organisms (Drosophila, zebrafish, C. elegans, etc.) are treated as research tools, not fields. This classification exists for browsing convenience and is not an official PubMed categorization.

Per-capita rates: numerator and denominator: The denominator is every tree member whose majorarea includes neuro; the numerator is those among them who served as last (corresponding) author of a CNS neuroscience paper in 2016–2025 (deduplicated). Cross-disciplinary CNS authors (no neuro tag in the tree, about three in ten of all matched last authors) are excluded from the numerator — they belong to other disciplinary populations, whose rates should be judged against their own population bases. Including them lowers the multiplier from 7.9 to 4.6 (a conservative lower bound); the conclusion is unchanged.

Known limitations: The Academic Family Tree (including Neurotree) is a crowdsourced database with incomplete coverage that skews toward elite lineages (this may inflate the 81%); name disambiguation is heuristic. This analysis is supporting data for an opinion Comment (submitted to Nature), not a peer-reviewed quantitative study.

Citation obligation: Use of the dataset requires citing Liénard, J.F., Achakulvisut, T., Acuna, D.E. & David, S.V. Nat. Commun. 9, 4840 (2018); the paper list is from PubMed/NCBI.
